SLIDE 04 SLIDE 05 • Big Idea & User Myth vs. Reality
Human Limitation → Design Response
SLIDE 04 • BIG IDEA

Human limitation → design response

HCI is not “making screens pretty”.

It is the discipline of shaping technology so that real people can perceive, understand and act with less unnecessary effort.

Every guideline has a psychological reason.

Visual hierarchy, feedback, chunking, undo, labels and grouping are not decoration; they compensate for predictable human constraints.

The question for every screen:

“What does the user need to see, remember, understand and recover from right now?”

SLIDE 13 • GESTALT 01

Proximity

Distance dictates relationship. The single most powerful layout principle.

SLIDE 13 • GESTALT 02

Similarity

Shared visual traits (color, shape, size) forge instant cognitive groupings.

SLIDE 14 • GESTALT 03

Closure

We automatically close open figures to perceive whole objects. (Basis of layered UI and card stacks, e.g., striped IBM logo, Photo Album stack).

SLIDE 14 • GESTALT 04

Continuity

We perceive continuous flow rather than disconnected segments. (Basis of scrollbars and sliders along a perceived continuous path).

SLIDE 15 • GESTALT 05

Figure/Ground

Separating the focus from the noise. The biological basis of the modal overlay (e.g., bright "Important Notice" dialog over dimmed backdrop).

SLIDE 15 • GESTALT 06

Common Fate

Elements that move together are instantly perceived as grouped. (e.g., dragging multiple selected files into a folder).

SLIDE 16 • THE INVISIBLE SKELETON OF UI

Good design feels “intuitive” because its visual structure perfectly maps to the brain's automatic grouping algorithms. Unintended relationships cause cognitive friction.

SLIDES 17–22 • Two Systems, One Eye: Foveal vs. Peripheral Vision 2-Degree Peephole

Two Systems, One Eye: Foveal vs. Peripheral Vision

Users do not see the whole screen with equal clarity. Detailed perception is concentrated around the current fixation point.

Dimension (Slide 17) Foveal Vision (Sharp Center) Peripheral Vision (Wide Awareness)
1. Visual Field (Slide 17 & 18) 1% of visual field (2 degrees) — Tiny central window around fixation 99% of visual field — Broad monitoring area outside fixation
2. Photoreceptors & Wiring (Slide 17 & 19) Cones (1:1 neural mapping) — Low convergence for precise signals; less pooling makes individual signals clearer Rods (compressed data) — Higher convergence & signal pooling improves sensitivity, reduces spatial precision
3. Resolution, Color & Speed (Slide 17 & 20) High-res, full color, slow — Answers “what exactly is it?” Requires direct gaze Low-res, grayscale, fast — Answers “did something change?” Fast motion/change detection
4. Primary UI Role (Slide 17 & 21) Inspection & Reading (Used after the eye lands on target): text, precise values, forms, icon meaning Motion detection & Guiding the fovea (Used before the eye lands): alerts, motion cues, state changes
SLIDE 18 • 1) VISUAL FIELD

• Foveal UI meaning: Use it for text, precise values, and detailed decisions.
• Peripheral UI meaning: Use it for simple cues that guide attention.

💬 Say: “Peripheral vision notices that something happened; foveal vision understands what it is.”

SLIDE 19 • 2) PHOTORECEPTORS & NEURAL WIRING

• Cone-Rich Center UI meaning: Small labels and exact symbols require fixation.
• Rod-Rich Periphery UI meaning: Peripheral cues should be large, simple, and salient.

💬 Say: “The center is optimized for precision; the periphery is optimized for sensitivity and awareness.”

SLIDE 20 • 3) RESOLUTION, COLOR, AND SPEED

• High-Res Inspection UI meaning: Use for instructions, labels, forms, numbers, and icon meaning.
• Fast Event Detection UI meaning: Use for alerts, motion cues, state changes, and notifications.

💬 Say: “Do not put detailed messages only in the periphery; use the periphery to pull attention to the detail.”

SLIDE 21 • 4) UI ROLE: INSPECTION VS. GUIDING FOVEA

• Inspection UI meaning: Make critical content readable and close to the user's task focus.
• Gaze Guidance UI meaning: Use motion sparingly; unnecessary animation steals attention.

📏 Design rule: “Peripheral signals should attract attention; foveal content should carry the detailed meaning.”

SLIDES 27–31 • PART 2: Attention & Memory Under Pressure ≈45 min + 10 min activity

PART 2: Attention & memory under pressure

Interfaces succeed when they reduce what users must notice, hold in mind and retrieve from scratch.

1. Attention is scarce and goal-directed.
2. Users often scan and satisfice rather than read everything.
3. Working memory is small and fragile.
4. Recognition is easier than recall.
SLIDE 28 • THE SCARCITY OF ATTENTION: THE COGNITIVE SIEVE

1. Habituation Filter

We reflexively dismiss what we see constantly (e.g., repeating error messages).

2. Attentional Blink Filter

For 0.15–0.45 seconds after spotting something important, we are temporarily blind to new stimuli.

3. Goal-Bias Filter

Blocks data irrelevant to the current task.

SLIDE 29 • GOAL-DRIVEN PERCEPTION

We Only See Our Goals (The Catch-All Drawer Experiment)

Adults are highly goal-driven; perception is severely filtered by the task at hand. In an interface, users only see what helps them achieve their immediate goal (like spotting only the wrench in a cluttered tool drawer).

SLIDE 30 • SATISFICING & SCENT

Users Don't Read. They Satisfice.

• SATISFICING: Clicking the first plausible option rather than evaluating all choices.
• INFORMATION SCENT: The literal, keyword-matching hunt. Everything else (like a $100 Prize banner while hunting Departments → Scholarships → Postgraduate) is invisible.

SLIDE 31 • CHANGE BLINDNESS

The Danger of the Shadows: Change Blindness

Never assume a user saw a change just because it happened on screen. (e.g., changing Flight Booking Cabin Class while the corner price silently jumps from $300 to $800).
✔ Critical updates must happen within the locus of attention.

SLIDE 32 • THE MEMORY MYTH

Memory is not a digital store (Not RAM or accumulators)

Memory formation is a physical change in neural patterns distributed across the brain, heavily reliant on context.

Short-Term RAM → Long-Term Disk
SLIDE 33 • THE ARCHITECTURE OF HUMAN MEMORY (EXAM TABLE)
Attribute 1. Sensory Memory 2. Working Memory (The Searchlights) 3. Long-Term Memory (The Warehouse)
Duration < 1 second. 20–30 seconds. Lifetime.
Capacity High, but volatile. Extremely limited (approx. 4 chunks). Practically infinite.
Mechanism Echoes of physical stimuli. The current, conscious focus of attention. Neural pattern activation; heavily context-dependent.
UI Implication Used for pre-attentive processing. Interfaces must show state. Do not require users to remember items. Retrieval is unreliable. Rely on Recognition over Recall.
SLIDE 34 • WORKING MEMORY CAPACITY

The Evolving Capacity of the Searchlights

  • • 1956: George Miller proposes the Magical Number 7 ± 2.
  • • Today: Modern research reveals that without rehearsal, working memory capacity is actually 4 ± 1 features or chunks.
  • • Working memory is even more constrained than early HCI pioneers believed.

SLIDE 36 • THE EXTERNALIZED MIND

Interface as an External Hard Drive

We do not rely on internal working memory; we mark up our environment. The interface acts as an external hard drive for the brain:

• Home > Registration > Step 3 of 25
• Cart: $45.99 (2 items) | Menu > Profile > Settings

⚠ Don't split a registration into 25 pages without context; users will lose track of where they are.

SLIDES 37–44 • The Golden Rule: Recognition > Recall HCI/Usability Heuristic
SLIDES 37 & 38 • THE GOLDEN RULE OF INTERFACE DESIGN

Recognition > Recall (Make objects, actions, and options visible)

Recall

High Load

Pulling a fact from the dark warehouse with no clues. High cognitive load, failure-prone.

  • • User must retrieve information from memory
  • • Higher cognitive load; slower and more error-prone
  • • Example: remembering a keyboard shortcut or command

Recognition

Low Load

Seeing an item and instantly matching it to a stored neural pattern. Fast, effortless, reliable.

  • • User sees cues and identifies the right option
  • • Lower cognitive load; faster and more reliable
  • • Example: choosing from a visible menu or toolbar
SLIDE 39 • EXAMPLE 1: SAVE ACTION

Save Action: Shortcut vs. Visible Toolbar

• Recall-based (Ctrl+S? Where is Save?): User must remember shortcut/command; easy to forget for novices; higher mental effort.

• Recognition-based (Visible Toolbar New | Open | Save | Print | Undo): User sees Save option immediately; guided by visible cues; faster for occasional and new users.

✔ Visible controls reduce memory burden and improve usability.

SLIDE 40 • EXAMPLE 2: LOGIN AND FORMS

Suggestions & Autofill Support Recognition

• Recall (Email: jo|): User must recall exact information; more typing and more errors; slower completion.

• Recognition (Dropdown john.doe@example.com): User recognizes the correct option from a list; less typing and fewer errors; faster completion.

✔ Autofill, suggestions, and history turn recall into recognition.

SLIDE 41 • MORE UI EXAMPLES OF RECOGNITION IN EVERYDAY INTERFACES
1. Recent Files Showing recent files is easier than asking users to remember exact file paths.
2. Menu Navigation Visible menus support recognition better than memorizing command names.
3. Icons + Labels An icon with a text label is easier to recognize than a hidden gesture.
4. Search Suggestions Autocomplete helps users recognize the intended term before finishing the query.

“Good interfaces remind users instead of testing their memory.”

SLIDE 42 • THE PARADIGM SHIFT: CLI VS. GUI
CLI (Recall) > rm -rf /old/folder • High cognitive load
• High error rate
• Steep learning curve
GUI (Recognition) Delete | Move | Trash 🗑️ • Low cognitive load
• Low error rate
• Shallow learning curve
SLIDE 43 • DESIGNING FOR RECOGNITION (3 TOOLS)
  • • Thumbnails: Visual recognition is insensitive to size. Tiny previews trigger instant recognition.
  • • Metaphorical Icons: Using pictures to convey function (⚙️, 🔍, ☁️). Bypasses language processing directly to feature recognition.
  • • Visual Context Cues: Color-coding or persistent avatars so users recognize exactly where they are (Dashboard, Projects).

SLIDES 45–50 • PART 3: Mental Models & The System Image ≈40 min + 10 min activity

PART 3: Mental models & human error

The product interface is the only bridge between designer intent and user understanding. (Slide 46: The Anatomy of an Interaction — And How to Save It).

1. Users act on the model they have, not the model designers intended.
2. The system image shapes the user's mental model.
3. Slips and mistakes need different design responses.
4. A robust interface expects human fallibility.
SLIDE 47 • A FUNDAMENTAL MISMATCH OF OPERATING SYSTEMS

The Human Mind

  • • Associative and heuristic-driven.
  • • Relies on partial models and superstition.
  • • Opportunistic and highly distractible.
  • • Forgives ambiguity.

Machine Logic

  • ■ Rigid and state-driven.
  • ■ Literal and precise.
  • ■ Requires uninterrupted sequential input.
  • ■ Demands absolute clarity.
SLIDE 48 • THE SOLE BRIDGE

Designers and users never actually communicate.

We cannot explain our intentions directly or sit next to the user. The sole bridge between the designer's intent and the user's understanding is the product itself.

SLIDE 49 • NORMAN'S SYSTEM IMAGE TRIANGLE

The System Image bears the entire burden of communication.

The user forms their mental model exclusively from interpreting the System Image. If the System Image is incoherent or contradictory, the user's mental model will be wrong.

SLIDE 50 • THERMOSTAT CASE STUDY

Users act on their models, even when they are entirely false.

✘ The False Mental Model (Valve Myth)

“Turn it up higher to heat the room faster!”

✔ The True Conceptual Model (Thermostat Switch)

“On/Off. Room reaches set limit, system cuts power.”

When the interface's conceptual model does not match the user's mental model, friction occurs. Good design either matches the user's existing model or clearly educates them on the new one.

SLIDES 51–56 • Diagnostics of Failure: Slips vs. Mistakes & Safety Nets High-Yield Exam Topic
SLIDE 51 • DESIGN STANCE

Do not stop at “user error”

Users are fallible in predictable ways.

They misread labels, forget modes, click adjacent controls, follow plausible but wrong models and miss unexpected updates.

Repeated errors are diagnostic data.

When several users make the same error, the interface is probably communicating the wrong model or failing to support the right action.

Design goal

Make the right action visible, the risky action harder, the system state legible and recovery possible.

SLIDE 52 • ERROR CLASSIFICATION MATRIX: SLIPS VS. MISTAKES (MEMORIZE FOR EXAM!)
Diagnostic Dimension Slip (Execution Failure) Mistake (Planning / Model Failure)
Intention / Goal Right Wrong
Action Performed Wrong Right (according to wrong plan)
User Expertise Often High / Experts (operating on autopilot) Often Low / Novices
Cognitive Stage Subconscious / Execution Conscious / Planning & Evaluation
SLIDE 53 • ANATOMY OF A SLIP

Right plan, derailed execution

  1. 1. Goal & Plan established: Holding milk carton & coffee cup.
  2. 2. Routine execution begins: Pouring milk into coffee cup.
  3. 3. Attention lapses → Wrong action performed: Putting the hot coffee cup into the refrigerator! ☕➡️🧊

The goal was correct. The plan was correct. But a subconscious lapse in attention caused the wrong action to be performed. Slips happen precisely because experts operate on autopilot.

SLIDE 54 • ANATOMY OF A MISTAKE

Flawed premise, perfect execution

🔍

Goal: FIND WORD — The user clicks the magnifying glass icon because they want to “Find” a word on the page. But the designer intended the icon to mean “Zoom In.”

The execution was flawless. The user did exactly what they intended. The failure occurred at the highest level of cognition: the mental model was wrong.

SLIDE 55 • DESIGNING FOR THE INEVITABLE

The true test of interaction design is how gracefully the system handles failure. We must build safety nets that anticipate the messy reality of human cognition.

SLIDE 56 • PREVIEW FOR WEEK 5: DESIGN RESPONSES WE WILL DEEPEN LATER

1. For slips: support correct execution

Spacing, clear target size, safe defaults, constraints, confirmations for destructive actions and undo.

2. For mistakes: repair the model

Use clearer terminology, better conceptual structure, informative feedback and explanations that match the user's task language.

3. For attention failures: move feedback to the locus of attention

Do not assume a change was noticed merely because it appeared somewhere on screen.

4. For memory limits: externalize state

Show what has happened, what is selected, what remains and how to continue.

SLIDE 57 • MASTER INTEGRATION MATRIX ★ Ultimate Exam Cheat-Sheet Table

From human constraint to interface decision

Complete synthesis of Week 2 connecting every human characteristic to its practical meaning and UI design response.

Human characteristic What it means in use Design response
1. Limited foveal vision Users scan instead of seeing everything equally. Create a strong visual hierarchy; put key actions where attention already is.
2. Gestalt grouping Users infer relationships from spacing, similarity and alignment. Group related controls; separate unrelated items; use continuity intentionally.
3. Color differences Some users cannot distinguish color-coded states. Use redundant cues: color + text + icon + shape/pattern.
4. Goal-directed attention Users miss unexpected changes outside their task focus. Place critical feedback near the action and make changes explicit.
5. Working memory limit Users cannot reliably hold many items for long. Chunk information; show state; avoid deep menu memory tasks.
6. Recall is hard Users struggle to reproduce commands/options from memory. Prefer visible choices, examples, defaults and recognition-based navigation.
7. Mental models Users act according to their internal explanation of the system. Create a coherent system image using labels, structure, feedback and behavior.
8. Slips vs. mistakes Execution failure differs from wrong intention/model. Use spacing/undo for slips; use explanation/feedback/model repair for mistakes.
