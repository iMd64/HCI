6.1 SLIDE 02 • THE PROCUREMENT TRAP

The Customer Who Pays ≠ The User Who Suffers

• The Recurring Trap (6.1 Slide 02): Assuming that the person buying the system shares the needs of the person operating it.

• The Result: Software that meets every procurement checklist but fails completely in the hands of the human worker.

6.1 SLIDE 03 • THE CATASTROPHIC COST OF ASSUMPTION
Dimension Assumption-Based Design Empirical-Based Design (UCD)
Team Behavior The team argues in a meeting room for hours about what the user wants. The team builds a prototype and observes a user attempting the task.
Decision Driver The loudest voice or the highest-paid person's intuition. Measured, observable human behavior.
Success Metric Shipping the code on the agreed-upon deadline. The user successfully and efficiently achieves their actual goal.
★ Key Definition (6.1 Slide 03): “UCD is the philosophical shift from guessing in a boardroom to measuring in reality.”

6.1 SLIDE 04 • INTUITION FIGHTS PHYSICAL REALITY

~9–10 mm Fingertip Patch vs. 2° Fovea & Fitts' Law

1. The Designer's Intuition: “These tightly packed buttons look sleek and save screen space.”
2. The Human Reality: Human vision is sharp only in the fovea (roughly 2 degrees), and physical fingers lack pixel-perfect precision (~9–10 mm contact patch).
3. The Law of Movement (Fitts' Law Preview): Time to target grows with distance and shrinks with target width. Tiny, close buttons are inherently slow and error-prone, regardless of how elegant they look in the design file.
6.1 SLIDE 05 • MAPPING THE HUMAN LANDSCAPE

The Stakeholder Onion (4 Concentric Rings)

Stakeholders are everyone affected by the success or failure of the system. The design team's goal is to navigate their conflicting needs.

1. Primary Stakeholders (Core) Hands directly on the interface (e.g., Data entry clerks).
2. Secondary Stakeholders Do not use the system directly, but receive its outputs or provide its inputs (e.g., Managers receiving reports).
3. Tertiary Stakeholders Affected by the success or failure of the system (e.g., Competitors, shareholders, patients).
4. Facilitating Stakeholders (Outer) Those who design, develop, and maintain the system.
6.1 SLIDE 06 • CRITICAL EXAM EXCEPTION

The Hierarchy of Needs is Not Always Linear

• General Rule: Design priority diminishes as you move outward from the primary user.

• The Exception (Hospital Life Support Machine):

  • — Primary Stakeholder (Medical Staff): Operates the control panel interface.
  • — Tertiary Stakeholder (The Patient in Bed): Holds the ultimate risk.
★ Exam Takeaway (6.1 Slide 06): The patient's (tertiary stakeholder's) need for absolute system reliability supersedes the primary user's need for a sleek interface.
6.1 SLIDE 07 • DIVERGENT INCENTIVES: THE STAKEHOLDER DIAGNOSTIC
Dimension Purchaser End-User
Primary Goal Minimize cost, maximize enterprise integration. Complete daily tasks with minimal cognitive load.
Time Horizon Long-term ROI and quarterly budgets. Immediate, second-by-second interaction.
Metrics for Success Feature completeness, security compliance, SLA uptime. Efficiency, error prevention, personal satisfaction.
The UX Danger System becomes a bloated checklist of unused features. System solves local problems but fails organizational scale.

“True design requires navigating this conflict without sacrificing the human at the screen.” (6.1 Slide 07)

6.1 SLIDES 08–15 • Waterfall Trap, ISO 9241-210 Whirlpool, 4 Gears, Delayed Discovery & True UCD Venn
6.1 SLIDE 08 • THE LINEAR TRAP OF TRADITIONAL ENGINEERING

✘ Why the ‘Waterfall’ Model Fails HCI

The traditional ‘waterfall’ model assumes human needs can be perfectly specified before a single pixel is drawn. It fails because human situations are complex, and designers are not infallible.

✕ LINEAR WATERFALL PIPELINE:
1. Requirements Specification ↘
2. Architectural Design ↘
3. Detailed Design ↘
4. Coding ↘
5. Integration ↘
6. Maintenance
6.1 SLIDE 09 • THE WHIRLPOOL: ISO 9241-210

✔ Evolutionary Cycles Replace Linear Pipelines

User-Centered Design is formalized in the ISO 9241-210 lifecycle. It replaces linear pipelines with evolutionary cycles (REFINEMENT and ITERATION).

6.1 SLIDES 10 & 11 • THE FOUR GEARS OF THE UCD ENGINE & THE CENTRAL AXIS
GEAR 1: CONTEXT OF USE

Understand who the users are, their explicit goals, and their physical/social environment.

GEAR 2: SPECIFY REQUIREMENTS

Translate context into measurable functional, data, environmental, and usability goals.

GEAR 3: PRODUCE SOLUTIONS

Move from conceptual models to concrete designs (sketches, wireframes, mockups).

GEAR 4: EVALUATE

Test the prototypes against the established requirements using real users.

6.1 SLIDE 11 • THE AXIS OF THE ENGINE: EMPIRICAL MEASUREMENT

Two non-negotiable commitments define UCD: (1) Early and continual focus on users, and (2) Empirical Measurement (decisions grounded in observed user behavior—where they click, where they hesitate, what errors they make—not intuition).

“Stopping at Design
is NOT UCD.” (6.1 S10)
6.1 SLIDES 12 & 13 • HILL-CLIMBING & THE COST OF DELAYED DISCOVERY

Why We Prototype Before Writing Production Code

• Hill-Climbing (6.1 Slide 12): Iteration is not a sign of failure; it is a hill-climbing approach (take a step/prototype → evaluate/test → adjust angle → step again toward the peak of usability).
• Delayed Discovery (6.1 Slide 13): Reactions must arrive while change is still cheap. A paper prototype discards a bad conceptual model in minutes; finding that error after coding costs orders of magnitude more.

6.1 SLIDES 14 & 15 • THE BREAKTHROUGH INSIGHT

3 Elements Required for True UCD

  • 1. Iteration without empirical measurement is just guessing twice.
  • 2. Stakeholder mapping without iteration means you only please the purchaser.
  • 3. True UCD requires all three operating simultaneously.
6.1 Slide 15: Build, measure, learn, and loop.
6.2 SLIDES 01–09 • Sub-Deck 6.2: Data Gathering in HCI (Say vs. Do Gap, Interviews & Questionnaires) WEEK 6.2 · SLIDES 01–21
6.2 SLIDES 01, 02 & 03 • THE CENTRAL DILEMMA OF USER RESEARCH

The Say vs. Do Gap & The Three Primary Methods

• Human beings are notoriously unreliable narrators of their own experiences.

• The Twinkling Staircase Study (6.2 Slide 02): Users reported that twinkling lights embedded in a staircase to encourage exercise significantly changed their behavior. The logged data, however, proved they did not.

• If we simply ask people what they do, they often give answers that show them in the best light, or simply misremember. Data gathering is the systematic process of discovering the ground truth.

1. INTERVIEWS (6.2 S03)

Deep qualitative insights. Probing the ‘Why.’

2. QUESTIONNAIRES (6.2 S03)

Scaling research. Measuring attitudes across populations.

3. OBSERVATION (6.2 S03)

Capturing ground truth. Watching the ‘How’ and ‘What.’

6.2 SLIDES 04, 05 & 06 • INTERVIEWS: SPECTRUM, 4-STEP ANATOMY & QUESTION ART
UNSTRUCTURED (Exploratory)

A conversation with a purpose. Open-ended questions. High discovery, low consistency. Hard to analyze.

★ SEMI-STRUCTURED (The Workhorse)

Pre-planned themes combined with organic probes. Balances structure with flexibility.

STRUCTURED (Standardized)

Fixed questions in a strict order. Closed questions. Easy to compare, but leaves no room for unexpected discoveries.

6.2 SLIDE 05 • ANATOMY OF THE SEMI-STRUCTURED INTERVIEW
Step 1: The Introduction — Establishing rapport, confirming consent, outlining purpose.
Step 2: The Warm-up — Simple, demographic, non-threatening questions to build confidence.
Step 3: The Main Session (Core) — Core thematic questions mapped to research goals.
Step 4: The Cool-down & Wrap-up — Summarizing, offering the participant the final word.
6.2 S06 • THE PITFALL

Leading Questions

“Don't you find this interface confusing?”

Pre-empts the answer and biases the participant. They may agree simply to please the investigator.

6.2 S06 • BEST PRACTICE

Neutral Probes & Silence

“Tell me more about the site layout...”

The hardest skill for a researcher is staying quiet. Silence forces the participant to fill the void with their own authentic reasoning.

6.2 SLIDES 07, 08 & 09 • QUESTIONNAIRES, LIKERT SCALES & CONTAMINATED SURVEY ERRORS
6.2 SLIDES 07 & 08 • QUESTIONNAIRES & LIKERT SCALES

• Shifts from “Why does this happen?” to “How many people feel this way?”
• Advantage: Reaches a wide, representative sample cheaply & asynchronously; quantifiable data.
• Vulnerability: Low response rates, sampling bias, and relies on self-reported ‘Say’ data.

1. Odd-Number Scale (5-Point with Neutral): The Odd-Number Safety Net — allows users to sit on the fence (Undecided/Neutral).
2. Even-Number Scale (4-Point without Neutral): The Even-Number Forced Choice — removes the neutral option, forcing a positive or negative lean.
6.2 SLIDE 09 • BAD INSTRUMENT DESIGN YIELDS CONTAMINATED DATA
Q1: “Is this app fast and easy to use?”

✘ Error: The Double-Barreled Question. What if it is fast but hard to use? Fix: Split into two questions.

Q2: “Age: 20–30, 30–40, 40–50.”

✘ Error: Overlapping Ranges. Where does a 30-year-old click? Fix: Make ranges mutually exclusive.

Q3: “How often: Frequently, Sometimes, Rarely.”

✘ Error: Ambiguous Scales. Subjective. Fix: Use concrete metrics (Daily, Weekly, Monthly).

6.2 SLIDES 10–21 • Observation, Contextual Inquiry, Indirect Logging, Ethics, Pilot Studies & Triangulation
6.2 SLIDES 10 & 11 • OBSERVATION: DECODING WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY DO

Controlled Precision (The Lab) vs. Ecological Validity (The Wild)

THE LAB • CONTROLLED USABILITY LAB (6.2 S11)

• Advantage: High precision. Eliminates distractions. Perfect for measuring specific task times, error rates, and micro-interactions.
• Vulnerability: Artificial. Users behave differently when they know they are being watched in a sterile room.

THE WILD • NATURAL SETTINGS / FIELD STUDIES (6.2 S11)

• Advantage: High ecological validity. Reveals true context, interruptions, and actual physical workarounds (e.g., sticky notes on a monitor).
• Vulnerability: High noise. Researcher has no control over external variables or interruptions.

6.2 SLIDES 12 & 13 • CONTEXTUAL INQUIRY: MASTER & APPRENTICE

“The User is the Master; the Researcher is the Apprentice”

• Traditional Interview: Investigator vs. User across a desk barrier → Power resides with the investigator.
• Contextual Inquiry: Researcher sits beside user at their real workstation → Power resides with the user.

1. Overview & Context: Build rapport and establish scope of the apprenticeship.
2. The Transition: Explicitly shift dynamic: “I'm going to watch you work now. Please think aloud.”
3. Observation & Interviewing (Core): User performs actual tasks; researcher observes, interrupts to clarify intent, and tests interpretations in real-time.
4. Wrap-up & Summary: Researcher summarizes understanding so user can correct misinterpretations.
6.2 SLIDE 14 • INDIRECT OBSERVATION

The Invisible, Continuous Investigator

  • • System Logging: Tracking clicks, navigation paths, and time-on-task.
  • • Web Analytics: Heatmaps, scroll depth, and drop-off funnels.
  • • Physical Tracking: Sensors monitoring crowd flow and physical bottlenecks.
⚠ The Catch (6.2 Slide 14): “This tells us exactly WHAT is happening, but never WHY.”
6.2 SLIDES 15, 16, 17 & 18 • RESEARCH ETHICS, DATA CUSTODY PIPELINE, GDPR & PILOT STUDIES
1. INFORMED CONSENT (6.2 S16)

Participants must know exactly what they are doing, how data is used, and their absolute right to withdraw at any time.

2. ANONYMIZATION (6.2 S16)

Stripping personally identifiable information (PII). Replacing names with codes before analysis begins.

3. SECURE STORAGE (6.2 S16)

Encrypted drives, separated physical records, and restricted access.

★ The Golden Rule of HCI Evaluation (6.2 Slide 16): “We are testing the design, not you.”
6.2 SLIDE 17 • GDPR & THE ‘LAPTOP ON THE TRAIN’ TEST

• GDPR Legal Framework: Mandates strict data minimization, informed consent, and user control.
• The Train Test: If you leave your research laptop on public transit—is the data encrypted? Are participant faces blurred? Are consent forms stored separately from raw data?

6.2 SLIDE 18 • THE NECESSITY OF PILOT STUDIES

“Never run a full study without testing the test.” A small trial run (often with peers) to check: (1) Ambiguous questionnaire instructions? (2) Does camera angle capture hands? (3) Does script take 60 min instead of 30? Fix the instrument before burning through your participant pool.

6.2 SLIDES 19, 20 & 21 • METHODOLOGICAL TRIANGULATION & THE METHODS MATRIX ★ Ground Truth = Aligning ‘Say’ + ‘Do’
Methodological Triangulation (6.2 Slide 19): Combining Interviews (The “Why”) + Surveys (The “How Many”) + Observation (The “What” / actual behavior) → Ground Truth.
Method (6.2 Slide 20) Data Type Scale / Reach Depth of Insight Bias Vulnerability Measures
Interviews Qualitative Low Deep Interviewer Bias ‘Say’
Questionnaires Quantitative High Shallow Sampling Bias ‘Say’
Lab Observation Quant / Qual Low High Precision Artificiality Bias ‘Do’
Field Observation Qualitative Low Contextual Depth Observer Effect ‘Do’
6.3 SLIDES 01–12 • Sub-Deck 6.3: The Sense-Making Phase (Requirements, Usability vs. UX Goals & Personas) WEEK 6.3 · SLIDES 01–21
6.3 SLIDES 01, 02, 03 & 04 • THE TRANSLATION GAP & ARCHITECTURAL GUARDRAILS

Crossing from Raw User Data to System Architecture

• The Translation Gap (6.3 Slide 02): Skipping the sense-making phase leads to products that engineers love to build, but nobody wants to use.

PILLAR 1: THE GUARDRAILS (6.3 S03)

Requirements & Goals. Defining what the system must do and how well it must perform.

PILLAR 2: THE PROTAGONISTS (6.3 S03)

Personas. Synthesizing data into evidence-based human archetypes.

PILLAR 3: THE ACTION (6.3 S03)

Interaction Models. Animating personas through Scenarios, Use Cases, Task Analysis, and User Stories.

Requirement Definition (6.3 Slide 04): A statement of what the product must do and how it must perform, before any implementation details are drawn.
✘ BAD REQUIREMENT (Implementation-Locked):

“The system needs a blue fingerprint scanner button on the home screen.”

✔ GOOD REQUIREMENT (Intent-Focused):

“The system must allow returning users to authenticate their identity securely in under 3 seconds.”

6.3 SLIDES 05 & 06 • THE 4 PILLARS OF REQUIREMENTS & UNPACKING THE ENVIRONMENT
1. FUNCTIONAL (6.3 S05)

What it does. (e.g., A robotic assembly arm must identify, manipulate, and weld metal accurately).

2. DATA (6.3 S05)

What it handles. Type, volatility, size, persistence, and accuracy (e.g., live stock-market feeds vs. persistent banking records).

3. ENVIRONMENTAL (6.3 S05)

Where it lives. The physical, social, organizational, and technical context of use.

4. USER (6.3 S05)

Who uses it. Novice vs. expert, frequency of use, cognitive and physical abilities.

6.3 SLIDE 06 • UNPACKING THE ENVIRONMENT (4 RINGS OF CONTEXT OF USE)
1. Physical Environment Lighting, noise, movement (e.g., thick protective gloves? glaring sunlight?).
2. Social Environment Collaboration & privacy (synchronous vs. asynchronous sharing? crowded public train?).
3. Organizational Env. Infrastructure & support (training protocols? company data policies?).
4. Technical Environment Ecosystem & constraints (bandwidth limits, cross-platform compatibility, legacy hardware).
6.3 SLIDES 07, 08 & 09 • OBJECTIVE USABILITY GOALS VS. SUBJECTIVE UX GOALS
1. Effectiveness Task success rates & accuracy.
Does it work?
2. Efficiency Time on task & resources expended.
Is it fast?
3. Safety Error prevention & recovery.
Protected?
4. Utility Feature coverage.
Right tools?
5. Learnability Time required for novices to achieve competence.
6. Memorability Retention of skills over time for intermittent users.
6.3 SLIDE 08 • SUBJECTIVE EMOTION: UX GOALS

• Negative: Boring, Frustrating, Annoying, Cutesy, Gimmicky, Creepy, Invasive, Patronizing.
• Positive: Satisfying, Enjoyable, Engaging, Empathetic, Exciting, Helpful, Motivating, Enhancing Sociability, Emotionally Fulfilling, Experiencing Flow.

“Usability asks: Can they use it?
UX asks: Do they want to?”
6.3 SLIDE 09 • THE USABILITY VS. UX 2×2 MATRIX
HIGH USABILITY / LOW UX: The ATM Machine

Highly efficient, perfectly safe, zero learning curve. But entirely devoid of joy, engagement, or emotional fulfillment.

HIGH USABILITY / HIGH UX ★ The Holy Grail of Interaction Design
[Low Usability / Low UX: System Failure]
LOW USABILITY / HIGH UX: Hardcore Video Game

Steep learning curve, intentionally inefficient, high error rate (dying in-game). Yet incredibly engaging, motivating, and immersive (Flow state).

6.3 SLIDES 10, 11 & 12 • THE ELASTIC USER, PERSONA DISTILLATION & ANATOMY OF A PERSONA
6.3 SLIDE 10 • THE ELASTIC USER

• The Fallacy: “If we make it generic enough, everyone will use it.”
• The Reality: Designing for “everyone” means designing for no one. It creates The Elastic User—an imaginary entity stretched to justify pre-made decisions.
• Rule: “If your user is ‘everyone,’ your product is a compromise.”

6.3 SLIDE 11 • THE DISTILLATION FUNNEL
  1. Step 1: Discover Patterns — Find behavioral trends in qualitative data.
  2. Step 2: Synthesize Characteristics — Group common goals, contexts, and pain points.
  3. Step 3: Forge the Archetype — Create a fictional but deeply accurate representative character.
★ Rule of Thumb (6.3 S11): 3–5 personas per product, with one primary persona whose needs win when trade-offs conflict.

6.3 SLIDES 13–21 • Scenarios, HTA, Use Cases, User Stories, Artifact Spectrum & Traceability Thread ★ Complete Interaction Modeling Suite
6.3 Slide 13 • The Narrative Shift: “A persona is a character. A requirement is the set design. Now, we need a plot.”
6.3 SLIDE 14 • SCENARIOS: CONTEXT & NARRATIVE

Informal, Context-Heavy Narrative

Communicates the “ideal state” and explores the problem space without committing to UI details. Focuses on daily life, triggers, emotional states, and external interruptions.

Scenario Example: The Grocery Delivery
• Stage/Trigger: Late afternoon, tired → Action: Checks app, orders essentials → Emotional State: Relieved, stressed about time → Environment: Home, cooking dinner, kids in background.
Filmstrip: [1. Notification arrives] → [2. Driver hands off groceries] → [3. Unpacks items, feeling organized]
6.3 SLIDE 15 • HIERARCHICAL TASK ANALYSIS (HTA)

Decomposing Goals into Subtasks & Logical Plans

Decomposing a high-level goal into subtasks, operations, and logical plans (e.g., ‘do 1-2-3; do 4 only if condition X is met’).

★ The Design Insight (6.3 Slide 15): HTA identified the post-completion error (users taking cash and leaving their card behind). Solution: Force Step 4 (Take Card) before Step 5 (Take Cash).
6.3 SLIDES 16, 17 & 18 • USE CASES (MAIN VS. ERROR FLOWS, SPECIFIC VS. ESSENTIAL) & USER STORIES
6.3 SLIDE 16 • USE CASES: STRUCTURE & LOGIC

Formal, step-by-step documentation of an interaction between an Actor and the System:

  • • Actor & Preconditions: Who initiates, and what must be true before starting?
  • • Main Success Flow: The “Happy Path” when everything goes right.
  • • Alternative / Error Flows: The edge cases (Conditions Not Met → Exit/Retry; Wrong Input → Prompt Correction; Network Drop → System Error → Exit).
Actor Initiates → Verify Preconditions → Main Flow → Process → Confirm Success
6.3 SLIDE 17 • SPECIFIC VS. ESSENTIAL USE CASES
Specific Use Case: “User taps the biometric scanner icon. System scans fingerprint.”
Problem: Locks design into mobile biometrics immediately. What if they use a desktop?
Essential Use Case: “User provides proof of identity. System verifies authentication.”
Benefit: Focuses purely on user intent & system responsibility.
6.3 SLIDE 18 • USER STORIES: THE AGILE UNIT Smallest unit of requirements for sprints
As a [role/persona]... I want to [specific goal]... so that [underlying benefit/value].

• Example (6.3 S18): “As a frequent traveler, I want to filter flights by carbon emissions so that I can reduce my environmental footprint.”
• Takeaway: Centers the developer's task entirely on human value, not just functional code.

6.3 SLIDES 20 & 21 • THE TRACEABILITY THREAD & ISO 9241-210 LOOP “Every line of code written should trace directly back to a human requirement.”
1. Requirement (System must handle rapid checkout)
2. Persona (Busy commuter who hates lines)
3. Scenario (Running late for train, needs coffee fast)
4. Use Case (System flow for 1-click mobile pre-order)
5. User Story (“As a commuter, I want to save my default payment...”)
★ 6.3 Slide 21 Final Rule: “Documentation is not a chore you finish; it is a hypothesis you test. The blueprints evolve as the building goes up.” (1. Understand Context → 2. Specify Requirements → 3. Produce Design Solutions → 4. Evaluate Against Requirements).
