SLIDES 05–07 • Stage 1: Touching the Glass, Touch Constraints & Haptic Feedback
SLIDE 05 • TOUCHING THE GLASS: EVOLVING DIRECT MANIPULATION

Why Direct Touch Reduces Anxiety & Increases Mastery

1. Absolute Positioning

1:1 mapping between the physical finger and the digital canvas.

2. Eliminating the Intermediary

No mouse, no cursor. The user feels they are directly manipulating the object.

3. Dynamic Fingertip Actions

Gestures like swiping, flicking, pinching, and dwelling replace rigid clicks.

Core Insight (Slide 05): Touch capitalizes on well-honed physical knowledge (holding, moving, stretching), reducing anxiety and increasing user mastery over the interface.
SLIDE 06 • THE CONSTRAINTS OF TOUCH: OCCLUSION AND DISCOVERABILITY
Physical Constraint SLIDE 06

1. Occlusion

Thumb physically hides data while touching. The user's own hand and finger block the exact target and surrounding UI feedback.

Cognitive Constraint SLIDE 06

2. The Discoverability Paradox

Gestures (like a three-finger swipe) feel natural once learned, but possess zero visual signifiers to tell a novice user they exist.

SLIDE 07 • FEELING THE DIGITAL: THE ROLE OF HAPTIC FEEDBACK

Smartwatch Hardware Actuation & Haptic Duality

SLIDE 07 • SMARTWATCH HAPTIC ANATOMY (5 COMPONENTS)
  • 1. Digital Crown Encoder: Captures physical rotation input.
  • 2. Micro-actuator Array: Distributed tactile actuators under display.
  • 3. Pressure Sensor Layer: Detects force applied to glass screen.
  • 4. Localized Vibration Wave Actuators: Generates crisp localized pulses.
  • 5. Taptic Engine Component: Linear resonant actuator creating physical click feel.
SLIDE 07 • 3-STEP HAPTIC ACTUATION PIPELINE
1. User Action Turning digital crown
2. System Actuation Triggers localized micro-vibration
3. Sensory Perception Brain registers a physical click
Cutaneous Haptics: Tactile sensations on the skin (buzzes and taps).
Kinesthetic Haptics: Perception of physical resistance and structural movement.
✔ Design Rule (Slide 07): Haptics must confirm actions without requiring visual attention, bridging the gap left by disappearing physical buttons.

SLIDE 09 • INTERFACE EVOLUTION: COMMAND VS. CONVERSATION
Dimension Command UI (Legacy VUI) Conversational AI (Modern)
Burden User must memorize exact rigid syntax. System infers and extracts user intent.
System Stance Reactive (waits for commands). Mixed-initiative (proactively asks clarifying questions).
Error Handling Fails completely on deviation (“I did not understand”). Tolerates interruptions and allows “barge-in” to correct course.
Flow Linear, step-by-step vocal menus. Dynamic, context-aware turn-taking.
SLIDE 10 • THE INVISIBLE UI PROBLEM

“What can I say?”

Unlike a GUI menu (where Play Music, Set Alarm, Settings are visibly listed), a pure Voice UI has no visual affordances or signifiers. It suffers from a severe discoverability crisis.

3 Design Solutions (Slide 10):
1. Conversational onboarding prompts.
2. Contextual suggestions (“You can also ask me to...”).
3. Multimodal integration (pairing voice with a peripheral screen).
SLIDE 11 • GESTURES IN SPACE

Interaction Without Surfaces

1. The Technology (MotionInput): Systems like MotionInput track hands, head, or full-body movements using standard cameras and ML algorithms, converting them into digital commands.
2. The ‘Why’: Essential for hands-busy, sterile (medical), or messy environments where physical touch is impossible or dangerous.
3. The Challenge (Midas Touch Problem): Delineating a deliberate pointing gesture from an unconscious hand wave during conversation.

SLIDE 13 • VIRTUAL REALITY (VR)

The Pursuit of Presence

1. The Goal — Presence: The psychological state where the brain accepts a synthetic environment as real, causing physical reactions to virtual stimuli.
2. The Constraint — Locomotion: How to move infinitely in the digital world while remaining physically stationary (e.g., standing in a small bedroom while walking through a cathedral).
3. The Risk — Motion Sickness: The sensory disconnect between the visual system (moving) and the vestibular system (stationary).
SLIDE 14 • AUGMENTED REALITY (AR)

The World as a Canvas

AR requires perfect spatial mapping. It relies on the system understanding depth, planes, and physical objects to anchor digital context seamlessly into reality.

SLIDE 16 • THE PARADIGM SHIFT: FROM EXPLICIT TO IMPLICIT INPUT

High Intention vs. Low Intention (Context-Aware)

In UbiComp, the computer infers intent from natural human physiology and routine, rather than waiting for a direct command.

PATH A • HIGH INTENTION (EXPLICIT INPUT)
User picks up remote → Presses button → TV turns on

Design Focus: Relies heavily on affordances and visible feedback.

PATH B • LOW INTENTION (IMPLICIT INPUT / CONTEXT-AWARE)
Walks into room → Wearable reads ID & heart rate → Lights, temp & music adjust

Design Focus: Shifts to proactivity, sensing, and user trust.

SLIDE 17 • CALM TECHNOLOGY

Ambient Computing & The Glowing Umbrella

  • • Peripheral Awareness: Information moves to the periphery of attention (not a noisy smartphone push notification).
  • • Ecologically Natural: The umbrella quietly glowing blue signals “it will rain today.” Digested instinctively when leaving the house without pulling out a screen.
  • • The Design Goal: Drastically decrease cognitive load while increasing environmental intelligence.
SLIDE 18 • MULTIMODAL MULTIPLIER

MIT Media Room “Put-That-There”

Action 1 (Voice): User says, “Move that...” (Concise, but ambiguous on its own).
Action 2 (Gesture): Points at a specific digital blue square on the wall (Resolves vocal ambiguity immediately).
Action 3 (Voice + Gesture): Says, “...there,” while pointing to a new location.

Multimodal interfaces cover each other's weaknesses, enabling flexible, highly efficient interaction.

SLIDE 19 • THE NUI MYTH

Natural ≠ Effortless

Don Norman: “A user interface isn't ‘natural’ just because it lacks a keyboard.”
  • • Swiping glass or talking to a plastic cylinder are heavily learned behaviors, not innate biological traits.
  • • “Natural User Interfaces” still require rigid constraints, clear feedback, and intentional onboarding.
  • • Conclusion: Do not conflate “invisible controls” with “easy to use.”

SLIDE 22 • THE ETHICS OF THE INVISIBLE INTERFACE

When Sensing Becomes Surveillance

1. The Loss of Consent: As interfaces disappear into the woodwork, so does the user's ability to explicitly consent to interaction.
2. Privacy by Default: How do you effectively ‘opt out’ of a room that is always listening and sensing?
3. The Designer's Burden: The exact same psychological insights used to remove friction can be weaponized into Dark Patterns and unauthorized biometric tracking.
SLIDE 23 • THE TECHNOLOGY CHANGES; THE PROCESS REMAINS

ISO 9241-210 User-Centered Design

Whether it is a CLI in 1980 or an Ambient AI in 2024, the fundamental HCI process of iterative, human-centered evaluation remains exactly the same.

1. Understand Context Who are the users, what is their task & environment?
2. Specify Requirements Define user needs & physiological constraints.
4. Evaluate Against Req. Test with real humans & iterate.
3. Design Solutions Build prototypes matching context & modalities.
SLIDE 24 • IN-CLASS ACTIVITY: STERILE OPERATION

Scenario: A hospital team needs to control a device during a sterile operation. Hands may be occupied, the room is noisy, and mistakes can be dangerous.

  1. Which style fits best: touch, voice, gesture, XR, or ambient? (Gesture + Haptic/Visual confirmation; noisy room hurts pure voice!)
  2. What is the biggest usability risk? (Accidental activation / Midas Touch or noise misrecognition)
  3. Which feedback channel would you add: visual, audio, haptic, or multimodal? (Multimodal: visual + haptic/auditory tone)
  4. How would you prevent accidental activation? (Explicit clutch gesture / dwell confirmation for destructive actions)

Chain: context of use → constraints → feedback → error prevention → user-centered evaluation.

SLIDES 27–32 • Good vs. Bad UI Critique Plates, Affordance/Mapping/Feedback & Non-Screen Interfaces
SLIDE 27 • GOOD EVERYDAY UI PATTERNS ✔ Clear & Forgiving
1. Mobile Checkout Form (1 Details → 2 Shipping → 3 Payment): • Clear labels: Each field (Name, Email, Card number) is clearly labeled so users know what to enter.
• Inline validation: Errors (Card number must be 16 digits) are shown right where they happen with helpful guidance.
• Simple flow: A clean step indicator shows progress through checkout.
2. Smart Home Living Room Tablet & Overview Dashboard: • High-contrast cards with explicit state labels (Lights: On, Thermostat: 72° Heating to 72°, Ceiling Fan: On, Window Shades: Open) and scannable KPI metrics (Total Users 12,480, Success Rate 98%).
3. Safe Error Recovery (✓ Item deleted — [Undo]): • Undo gives users a safe way to reverse actions.
SLIDE 28 • BAD EVERYDAY UI ANTI-PATTERNS ✘ High Friction & Risk
1. Too many options (Cluttered Kiosk): The kiosk screen is cluttered with dozens of tiny buttons, making it hard to find what you need.
2. Hard to read and use (Low-Contrast Mobile Form): Low contrast, tiny text and small touch targets make the form difficult to read and complete on a mobile device.
3. Confusing interface (Unlabeled Thermostat Icons): Important controls use unfamiliar, unlabeled icons (21° with cryptic symbols), making it unclear how to change the settings.
4. Destructive action with no confirmation ([Delete]): The file is permanently deleted with a single click, with no warning or chance to undo.
