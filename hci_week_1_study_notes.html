SLIDE 03 • Source: Rogers et al. Ch. 1
Core Definition & 3 Pillars

What is HCI?

A discipline for designing, evaluating, and implementing interactive systems

SLIDE 03 DIAGRAM • THE 4-ELEMENT HCI CHAIN
★ Core Principle (Slide 03): “HCI asks how to make technology fit people, instead of forcing people to fit technology.”
1. DESIGN

create possible interfaces

2. EVALUATION

test whether people can use them

3. IMPLEMENTATION

build interactive systems for human use

SLIDE 04 • Source: Rogers et al. Ch. 1
The recurring vocabulary for the whole course

Three questions every interface answers

Question 1: Can? USABILITY

Usability

Can people use it effectively, efficiently, and with satisfaction?

Question 2: Need? USEFULNESS

Usefulness

Does it solve a real need or support a meaningful goal?

Question 3: Feel? USER EXPERIENCE (UX)

User Experience

How does it feel: trustworthy, frustrating, engaging?

★ Slide 04 Synthesis Rule: “Good HCI lives where all three overlap.”

SLIDE 09 • Source: Week 1 notes + Norman Ch. 1
Real-World Costs of Poor Interaction Design

Why usability matters

Poor interaction design has real costs

• Civic / Voting: Confusing ballots can affect real outcomes
• Healthcare: Medical device interfaces can contribute to dosage errors
• Critical Systems: Control-room design can influence incident response
✔ Positive Design Goal (Slide 09): “A well-designed product lowers the barrier between intention and action.”
Time

slow workflows, repeated errors

Money

abandoned products, support calls

Trust

user confidence eroded by confusing behavior

Safety

medical, industrial, transport risk

★ Slide 09 Bottom Line: “Usability matters because it shapes outcomes, not just opinions.”
SLIDE 10 • Source: Norman, DOET Ch. 1
Norman's Everyday Things

Blame the design, not the user

Norman's lesson: users act on the signals the design gives them

1. CONFUSING DOOR SIGNALS

Door handles can suggest “pull” even when the door requires “push.”

2. STOVE KNOB MAPPING

Stove controls can fail when knob layout does not map naturally to burners.

Diagnostic Principle (Slide 10): “When people repeatedly make the same ‘mistake,’ look for a design signal that invites it.”
Good Design Rule (Slide 10): “Good design makes the correct action visible, natural, and easy to recover from.”

SLIDE 11 • In-Class Activity: 5-Minute Design Critique Turn everyday frustration into HCI evidence
STEP 1 • CHOOSE

a confusing app, kiosk, door, form, remote, or appliance

STEP 2 • OBSERVE

what the design seems to invite users to do

STEP 3 • DIAGNOSE

which goal fails: usability, usefulness, or UX

STEP 4 • REDESIGN

one small change that would reduce the error

📸 Before next class (Slide 11): Take one photo of a bad everyday design and be ready to explain why it fails.
