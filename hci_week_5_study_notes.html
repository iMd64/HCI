SLIDE 04 • THE DUAL PILLARS OF INTERACTION

Discoverability vs. Understanding

PILLAR 1 • DISCOVERABILITY SLIDE 04

“What actions are possible, and where do I perform them?”

  • • Smartphone Lock Screen Example: Visual cues highlight ACTIONABLE REGION DETECTED, bottom WAKE GESTURE AFFORDANCE (^ bar), and the corner Camera icon.
✔ Rule (Slide 04): Discoverability prevents the user from having to guess or blindly tap.
PILLAR 2 • UNDERSTANDING SLIDE 04

“How does this work, and what do the controls mean?”

  • • Commercial Espresso Machine Example: Clear LOGICAL GROUPINGS IDENTIFIED, SYSTEM STATUS MONITORING (pressure gauges), BREW GROUP ACTIVATION buttons aligned above each brew head, and STEAM & HOT WATER CONTROLS mapped to the steam wand.
✔ Rule (Slide 04): Understanding prevents catastrophic errors by aligning the system's logic with human expectations.
SLIDES 05–11 • Part I: Norman's Design Vocabulary (Affordances, Signifiers, Mappings & Constraints)
SLIDE 05 • AFFORDANCE: THE INVISIBLE POTENTIAL

What Actions Are Physically or Digitally Possible

Definition (Slide 05): “The relationship between the physical properties of an object and the capabilities of the interacting agent that determine how the object could possibly be used.”

EXAMPLE 1 • CHAIR

“Affords support/sitting (for a human).”

EXAMPLE 2 • HEAVY CRATE

“Affords lifting (but only for a strong agent). If the user is weak, the affordance does not exist.”

EXAMPLE 3 • TABLET GLASS

“Affords touching and swiping (requires physical contact capabilities).”

★ Key Rule (Slide 05): Affordances define what actions are possible. They exist even if they are invisible to the user.
SLIDE 06 • SIGNIFIER: THE VISIBLE COMMUNICATION

Where and How the Action Should Take Place

Definition (Slide 06): “Any mark, sound, or perceivable indicator that communicates appropriate behavior to a person. Signifiers tell us where the action should take place.”

1. MAP ZOOM BUTTONS (+ / −)

The entire screen affords touching, but the ‘+’ and ‘−’ icons signify where to tap to zoom.

2. FOOTPRINTS IN SNOW

The footprints of previous walkers are accidental, yet powerful signifiers of the best path.

3. ELEVATOR BUTTON ‘4’ + BRAILLE

The physical button affords pushing; the raised Braille and printed numbers signify which button to push.

★ Key Rule (Slide 06): “In design, signifiers are more important than affordances. They are the communication layer.”
SLIDE 07 • DIAGNOSTIC MATRIX: AFFORDANCE VS. SIGNIFIER Video cue: Norman_s_Design_Vocabulary.mp4 (8:53)
Dimension Affordance Signifier
Definition The potential relationship between object and agent. The perceivable cue that communicates the affordance.
Primary Function Determines what actions are possible. Communicates where and how to perform the action.
Nature of the Concept A physical or programmed reality (can be invisible). A communication mechanism (must be perceivable).
Digital Example (The Submit Button) The invisible 44×44 pixel clickable hit box coded in HTML. The drop-shadow, rounded corners, and the word Submit rendered on screen.
SLIDES 08 & 09 • MAPPING: SPATIAL CORRESPONDENCE & STOVE AUTOPSY

Natural Mapping vs. Arbitrary Mapping

Definition (Slide 08): “The relationship between a set of controls and the devices or actions they dictate. Natural mapping takes advantage of spatial analogies to yield immediate understanding.”

SLIDE 08 • CAR SEAT CONTROL

Best Spatial Mapping

“Controls are arranged in the exact spatial configuration as the object being controlled.”

SLIDE 09 • ARBITRARY MAPPING

✘ Crossed & Ambiguous

Requires conscious cognitive effort, memorization, or reading tiny labels. Prone to severe errors under stress.

SLIDE 09 • NATURAL MAPPING

✔ Spatial Alignment

Exploits spatial analogies. Zero cognitive load. Immediate understanding.

SLIDES 10 & 11 • CONSTRAINTS: THE POWER OF LIMITATION & THE 4 PILLARS

Making Incorrect Actions Impossible or Unlikely

Definition (Slide 10): “Physical, logical, semantic, and cultural limits that prevent wrong actions. Constraints relieve the user's short-term memory by making incorrect actions impossible or highly unlikely.”

SLIDE 10 • PHYSICAL CONSTRAINT (USB-A PORT)

⚠ BLOCKER — ORIENTATION MISMATCH: PHYSICALLY RESTRICTED. Physical geometry blocks upside-down insertion.

SLIDE 10 • DIGITAL EQUIVALENT (CONTEXT MENU)

Deactivating invalid options based on current state (e.g., Cut, Copy, Delete active; Paste, Group, Ungroup grayed out).

✔ Rule (Slide 10): “Good constraints make the desired action obvious and the incorrect action impossible. To err is human; design should anticipate it.”
SLIDE 11 • THE FOUR PILLARS OF CONSTRAINT (PHYSICAL WORLD VS. DIGITAL WORLD)
Constraint Pillar Core Mechanism (Slide 11) Physical World Example Digital World Example
1. Physical Relies on physical properties to restrict action. SIM Card: Only Fits One Way (BLOCKER: PHYSICALLY NOTCH — Tray restricts orientation). UI Slider (Volume 100%): Physically cannot be dragged past 100%.
2. Logical Uses deductive reasoning to determine alternatives. Leftover Bolt: Deductive reasoning during shelf assembly — “Something's Wrong”. Graying out the Next button until all mandatory form fields are filled (INACTIVE: MANDATORY FIELDS EMPTY).
3. Semantic Relies on the meaning of the situation to control options. Motorcycle Windshield: Meaning of riding forward — must sit in front of rider to block forward wind. Digital avatar builder where a hat can only be placed on a head, not a foot (INVALID PLACEMENT: HAT FOR HEAD ONLY).
4. Cultural Relies on accepted cultural conventions. Red Traffic Light: STOP: RED LIGHT UNIVERSAL DANGER/STOP. Placing the Close Window ‘X’ in the top right corner of a screen (CLOSE WINDOW 'X': CULTURAL CONVENTION).
SLIDES 12–18 • Feedback, Conceptual Models, System Image, 7 Stages of Action & Gulfs
SLIDE 12 • FEEDBACK SHOULD MATCH THE ACTION

Avoid Treating 0.1 Seconds as a Universal Hard Limit

• IMMEDIATE FEEL

Very short response times support direct manipulation.

• PROGRESS FEEDBACK

Longer operations should show meaningful status or progress.

• ACTION OUTCOME

Users must know whether the action succeeded, failed, or needs attention.

SLIDE 13 • THE CONCEPTUAL MODEL

Thermostat: User's Valve vs. System's Binary Switch

Definition: A highly simplified, internal explanation of how a system works. It doesn't have to be physically accurate, it just has to be useful. When a system's conceptual model matches the user's mental model, interaction feels intuitive.

User's Model: The Valve

“It's like a valve. If I turn it to 90 degrees, it will heat the room faster!”

System's Model: Binary Switch

System Logic: Full power (ON/OFF) until target temp is reached, then off.

“Users act on their models. If the design projects the wrong model, inevitable mistakes will follow.” (Slide 13)
SLIDE 14 • THE BURDEN OF THE SYSTEM IMAGE

Why Designers Cannot Speak Directly to Users

The only way the Designer can communicate their Conceptual Model to the User is through the System Image. Affordances, signifiers, constraints, and mappings are the vocabulary of this image.

SLIDES 16 & 17 • THE ABYSSES OF INTERACTION & BRIDGING THE GULFS

Engineering the Two Bridges Between User and System

1. THE GULF OF EXECUTION (Slide 16)

The gap between what the user wants to do and what the interface lets them express.

Diagnostic Question: “How do I work this?”

Bridging Execution (Slide 17): Feedforward answers: “What can I do?”
4 Pillars: Affordances, Signifiers, Constraints, Mappings.
2. THE GULF OF EVALUATION (Slide 16)

The gap between the system's state and the user's ability to perceive/understand it.

Diagnostic Question: “What just happened? Is it working?”

Bridging Evaluation (Slide 17): Feedback answers: “What happened?”
2 Pillars: Feedback, Conceptual Models.

SLIDES 19–33 • Part II: Jakob Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics & Heuristic Evaluation Video cue: HCI__10_Usability_Heuristics.mp4 (8:13)
SLIDES 19, 20 & 21 • THE DIAGNOSTIC TOOLKIT: 10 LENSES OF INTERACTION Nielsen & Molich (1990 / 1994)

Introduced by Jakob Nielsen and Rolf Molich in 1990 (refined in 1994), these are not rigid laws. They are heuristics—broad rules of thumb and general principles that guide design decisions and structure system critiques.

• TARGET (Slide 20) Find likely usability problems systematically.
• EVIDENCE (Slide 20) Identify the visible issue and the affected task.
• LIMIT (Slide 20) A heuristic inspection does not replace observation of real users.
HEURISTIC #1 SLIDE 22

1. Visibility of System Status

Always keep users informed about what is going on through appropriate feedback within a reasonable time.

SYMPTOM: Unlabeled spinner → “Am I frozen? Did it crash?”
CURE: Uploading Files... 68% Complete • Est: 12s [Cancel]
Diagnostic Q: Does the user know where they are, what the system is doing, and how long it will take?
HEURISTIC #2 SLIDE 23

2. Match Between System and Real World

Speak the user's language with familiar words, phrases, and concepts rather than system-oriented terms. Follow real-world conventions.

SYMPTOM: Exception Error 0x80040154: Class not registered. Terminate process? (Alien language!)
CURE: Desktop folders & Trash Can icon (Familiar metaphor.)
Diagnostic Q: Does the interface force the user to learn technical jargon, or does it map to their existing mental model of the world?
HEURISTIC #3 SLIDE 24

3. User Control and Freedom

Users often choose system functions by mistake. They need a clearly marked “emergency exit” to leave the unwanted state without an extended dialogue. Support undo and redo.

SYMPTOM: Step 4 of 5: Review Order with only a [Next] button (Trapped! No Back/Cancel).
CURE: Gmail toast: Message Sent [Undo].
Diagnostic Q: If the user misclicks, how painful is the recovery?
HEURISTIC #4 SLIDE 25

4. Consistency and Standards

Users should not have to wonder whether different words, situations, or actions mean the same thing. Follow platform conventions.

SYMPTOM: Non-standard button placement & styling ([Save] [Cancel] swapped — Fights platform standards!)
CURE: Standard top-left back arrow ← and top-right + (Expected placement.)
Diagnostic Q: Does this design reinvent the wheel, forcing the user to learn a new interaction pattern for a standard task?
HEURISTIC #5 SLIDE 26

5. Error Prevention

Even better than good error messages is a careful design that prevents a problem from occurring in the first place. Eliminate error-prone conditions or use constraints.

SYMPTOM: Free-text input Departure Date: [March 5th] (Guaranteed formatting error.)
CURE: Structured calendar date picker (Constrained to valid choices.)
Diagnostic Q: Does the interface allow the user to make an invalid choice, or does it constrain them to only valid actions?
HEURISTIC #6 SLIDE 27

6. Recognition Rather Than Recall

Minimize memory load by making objects, actions, and options visible. The user should not have to remember information from one part of the dialog to another.

SYMPTOM (Recall): Terminal $ tar zxvf archive.tar.gz (Requires rote memorization.)
NUANCE (Slide 27): 200-country dropdown: “Recognition isn't always best! Predictive typing (recall + recognition) wins here.”
Diagnostic Q: Are options visually present, or is the user relying on their fragile working memory to proceed?
HEURISTIC #7 SLIDE 28

7. Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

Accelerators—unseen by the novice user—may often speed up the interaction for the expert user. Allow users to tailor frequent actions.

SYMPTOM: Rigid 8-step Setup Wizard [Next >] (Slows down experts.)
CURE: Menu bar with shortcuts (⌘N, ⌘T, ⌘S, ⌘P — Accelerators for power users.)
Diagnostic Q: Does the interface support both the slow, guided walk of a beginner and the rapid, customized sprint of an expert?
HEURISTIC #8 SLIDE 29

8. Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Dialogs should not contain information that is irrelevant or rarely needed. Every extra unit of information competes with the relevant units and diminishes visibility.

SYMPTOM: Cluttered portal (WIN A FREE IPOD! CLICK HERE! — Irrelevant noise.)
CURE: Clean Google Search home page (High signal-to-noise ratio.)
Diagnostic Q: Can any visual element be removed without reducing the user's understanding of the task?
HEURISTIC #9 SLIDE 30

9. Recognize, Diagnose, & Recover from Errors

Error messages should be expressed in plain language (no codes), precisely indicate the problem, and constructively suggest a solution.

SYMPTOM: Blank 404 Not Found page (Dead end. Blames the user.)
CURE: Plain explanation + [Go to Homepage] [Search the Site] [Contact Support] (Actionable recovery paths.)
Diagnostic Q: Does the error message blame the user, or does it offer a clear, actionable path forward?
HEURISTIC #10 SLIDE 31

10. Help and Documentation

Even though it is better if the system can be used without documentation, it may be necessary to provide help. It should be easy to search, focused on the user's task, and concise.

SYMPTOM: User Manual: 500 Pages (Nobody reads this.)
CURE: Inline popover tooltip on ? icon (Contextual & immediate.)
Diagnostic Q: Is help contextual, appearing at the exact moment and place the user needs it?

SLIDE 33 • CRITICAL DISTINCTION

Heuristic Evaluation ≠ Usability Testing

Both are useful; neither replaces the other.

• HEURISTIC INSPECTION Evaluators inspect a design against established guidelines.
• USABILITY TESTING Representative users attempt representative tasks; we observe outcomes.
• WEEK 9 PREVIEW Evaluator independence, severity ratings, study design, and reporting.

GOLDEN RULE #1 SLIDE 36

1. Strive for consistency.

Action sequences, layouts, and terminology must be predictable to leverage existing mental models.

Submit Submit Submit
GOLDEN RULE #2 SLIDE 36

2. Cater to universal usability.

Design for a spectrum of users—provide walk-up-and-use menus for novices, but enable shortcuts/macros for experts.

File ▸ Save File (Novice Menu) Ctrl + S (Expert)
GOLDEN RULE #3 SLIDE 37

3. Offer informative feedback.

Every action demands a perceivable system response proportional to the magnitude of the action.

User ──(Action)──► System ──(Reaction)──► User
GOLDEN RULE #4 SLIDE 37

4. Design dialogs to yield closure.

Organize sequences into beginning, middle, and end. Closure provides psychological relief and readies the user for the next group of actions.

Cart → Shipping → Payment → Confirmation: ✓ Success! Receipt Emailed. (100%)
GOLDEN RULE #5 SLIDE 38

5. Prevent errors.

Do not rely on users being careful. Anticipate motor slips via spacing and constraints. Mitigate cognitive mistakes by graying out invalid options and demanding confirmation for destructive acts.

Confirm Database Deletion
Type database name: Confirm DeleteDB
Cancel Delete
GOLDEN RULE #6 SLIDE 39

6. Permit easy reversal of actions.

Relieves user anxiety and encourages active exploration. When users know they can always return to a previous state, they bypass the paralyzing fear of making a mistake.

INITIAL STATE (CLEAN UI) ⇄ UNDO / Trial & Error ERROR STATE (MESSY UI)
GOLDEN RULE #7 SLIDE 40

7. Support internal locus of control.

Users want to be the initiators of actions, not the responders to system demands. Avoid surprising system updates, unprompted mode shifts, or forced navigation.

✘ SYSTEM IN CONTROL (PASSIVE)
✔ USER IN CONTROL (AUTONOMOUS)
GOLDEN RULE #8 SLIDE 41

8. Reduce short-term memory load.

Respect the 4-chunk, 20-second limit. Displays must be kept simple. Information from one part of a dialog must remain visible in the next. Let the computer remember, so the human doesn't have to.

WORKING MEMORY TUBE (4 CHUNKS) EXTERNALIZED VIA:
1. BREADCRUMBS 2. PERSISTENT SEARCH TERMS 3. VISIBLE STATE INDICATORS

SLIDE 48 • DEBRIEF: EXAMPLE ENGINEERING FINDINGS Problem → Principle → Redesign
1. AMBIGUOUS PATIENT (A. K. | ID: 1945?) SLIDE 48

Principle: Recognition / error prevention → Redesign: Clear identity confirmation (full patient name, DOB, verified MRN, and photo/wristband match).

2. DOSE WITHOUT UNITS (Dose: 10, Frequency: 2) SLIDE 48

Principle: Constraints / match to real world → Redesign: Explicit validated units (constrained dropdowns for mg/mL, explicit route IV/PO, and frequency every 12 hours).

3. CONFUSING ACTIONS ([CANCEL] [SAVE] identical) SLIDE 48

Principle: Consistency / signifiers → Redesign: Clear primary and secondary actions (distinct visual hierarchy, spacing, and confirmation step).

4. UNCLEAR SAVED STATE (Order successfully saved (?)) SLIDE 48

Principle: Feedback / visibility → Redesign: Unambiguous success or failure (explicit timestamped state and closure).
